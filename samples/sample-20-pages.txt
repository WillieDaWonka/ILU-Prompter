## Welcome

Good evening, everyone.

Please take a seat, find a cup of tea if you can, and let us begin.

My name is Marguerite, and I have the honour of chairing the Riverside Community Garden Trust. Tonight is our annual meeting, and it is the one evening of the year when we all sit in the same room and look at the same pictures of the same muddy boots.

Thank you for coming. I know it is a wet night. I know the football is on. I know there is a perfectly good sofa waiting for you at home. The fact that you are here says something about this place, and about the people who love it.

Let me tell you how the evening will go.

First, I will look back over the year. I will keep it short, and I will keep it honest, which means you will hear about the carrots that failed as well as the pumpkins that did not.

Then we will talk about the money. Do not worry. There will be no complicated charts. There will be one chart, and it has a very simple shape.

After that, we will hear about our new greenhouse, about the children who visited us from the local schools, and about the people who give their Saturday mornings so that the rest of us can eat a good tomato in August.

We will finish with our plans for next year, and with a few thank yous that are long overdue.

There will be time for questions at the end. If you have a question about slugs, please know that I have been waiting all year for someone to ask.

A quick word about the room. The exits are at the back, on both sides. The toilets are down the corridor on your left. The kettle is in the corner, and the biscuits are on the table beside it. Please do not take all of the ginger ones. I am watching.

If you need a seat nearer the front, or you cannot hear me well, just raise a hand and one of our stewards will help. They are the people wearing the green badges, and they are very friendly.

Before I go any further, I want to say something that I think matters more than any number I will read out tonight.

Nine years ago, this was a patch of broken concrete behind the old bus depot. There were bricks, there were bottles, and there was a shopping trolley that nobody could explain.

Today there are one hundred and twelve growing beds. There is an orchard with forty young trees. There is a pond with frogs in it, and a bench where people sit at lunchtime and say nothing at all, which is sometimes the best thing a garden can give you.

None of that happened by accident. It happened because ordinary people turned up, again and again, with spades and flasks and stubbornness.

So, before we start the formal business, I would like to ask you to do one small thing.

Look to your left. Look to your right. Look behind you if you are brave.

Those are the people who did this. Give them a round of applause.

Thank you. Now, let us begin.

## The year in review

Let me take you back to the first week of January.

It was cold. It was properly cold, the kind of cold that makes the soil ring when you tap it with a fork. Our faithful Saturday team came anyway, in woolly hats and two pairs of socks, and spent the morning mending the fence along the east side.

I mention the fence because it is a good example of how this garden works. Nobody asked them to do it. Nobody put it on a list. Somebody noticed a gap, and somebody else said, I have some wire in the garage, and by lunchtime the gap was gone.

That is the spirit of the whole year in one small story.

In February we ordered seeds. We always order too many. This year we ordered enough tomato seeds to plant the entire county, and we had to hold a seed swap just to get rid of them. Forty people came, which tells you how many tomatoes this town really wants.

March was the month of mud. We had more rain than anyone could remember, and for three weeks the paths were more like streams. We laid wooden boards across the worst of it, and the children called them the bridges of doom. I am happy to report that nobody fell in, although one very determined spaniel came close.

April is when the garden wakes up. The blossom came out on the orchard trees, only a little, because the trees are still young, but enough to make us stand about with our mouths open. We counted eleven kinds of bee. I did not know there were eleven kinds of bee. I now have opinions about all of them.

In May, we opened the gates to the public for the first time that year. Over four hundred people walked through in a single weekend. Some came to buy plants, some came for the cake, and some came because they had walked past for years and never known what was behind the wall.

I think that last group is the most important. A garden that nobody knows about is only a private hobby. A garden that the whole street knows about is a public good.

June and July were the long, bright months. The beans climbed, the courgettes took over, and we gave away more vegetables than we could count. Our little shed by the gate, where anyone can take what they need and leave what they can, was emptied and refilled every single day. We think it fed at least thirty families through the summer. We cannot be exact, because nobody signs for a courgette, and that is exactly how it should be.

August brought the heat, and with it, the great water shortage. I will talk more about that later, because it taught us something I do not want us to forget.

September was harvest. The pumpkins did well, as promised. The apples did not, as feared. The carrots, I am sorry to say, came up the size of a thumb. We held a funeral for them. There were speeches.

In October we closed the summer beds, spread the compost, and planted the garlic. In November we held our bonfire night, and nobody lost an eyebrow, which is a record.

And in December, we did something we had never done before. We stayed open. A handful of volunteers kept the winter garden going, with cabbages and kale and leeks, and a small stall of hot soup on the last Saturday before the holiday. We sold out in an hour.

So that was the year. It was wet, it was hot, it was muddy, it was sweet. If I had to give it one word, I would say it was ours.

Let us look at what we actually grew.

## Spring planting

Now I want to spend a little time on spring, because spring is where the work is won or lost.

Anyone who has ever tried to grow anything knows that the secret is not the planting. The secret is the waiting.

Every year, on the first warm day in March, somebody comes through those gates with a tray of tender seedlings and a look of pure joy. And every year, I have to say the same thing to them. Please put those back. It is still too early. There is a frost coming, and the frost does not care how excited you are.

This year, we tried something new. Instead of guessing, we kept a record.

Our volunteer Anwar, who is an engineer in his working life, fixed a small thermometer to a post near the pond and wrote down the lowest temperature every morning. He did this for one hundred and eighty days. He did it in the rain. He did it on his birthday.

At the end of it, he gave us a simple chart, the very one I promised you earlier. It shows the last frost of the season falling on the ninth of May, nearly three weeks later than the old rule of thumb we had all been using.

Think about what that means. If we had planted on the day we always planted, we would have lost nearly every bean.

Because of Anwar, we waited, and because we waited, we lost almost nothing. So the next time you see a man standing by a post with a notebook, please be kind to him. He may be saving your dinner.

Let me tell you how we organise the planting itself, for those of you who are new.

We divide the garden into four zones. The first zone is for roots, such as carrots, beetroot and parsnips. The second is for leaves, such as lettuce, spinach and chard. The third is for fruits, which in gardening means beans, tomatoes, squash and anything else with seeds on the inside. The fourth zone is for flowers and herbs, which are there to feed the bees and the cook.

Each year, the zones move round by one. The roots go where the leaves were, the leaves go where the fruits were, and so on. This keeps the soil healthy and stops the pests from settling in.

It sounds simple, and it is. But it needs a plan, and a plan needs a map, and a map needs someone to draw it.

That someone was Priya, who spent six evenings with a ruler and a box of coloured pencils and produced the most beautiful planting plan I have ever seen. It is pinned up in the shed. If you have not looked at it, please do. It is very nearly a work of art.

We also learned a few things the hard way.

We learned that sweetcorn needs to be planted in blocks and not in rows, because it relies on the wind to carry its pollen. Our first attempt, in a single long line, produced cobs with only a handful of kernels on them. They looked like a very sad set of teeth.

We learned that a bed of lettuce left in full sun in July will bolt faster than you can say salad. Next year we will give it shade.

We learned that onions do not like to be fussed over. Leave them alone, and they will reward you.

And we learned that you can never, ever have too much compost.

Speaking of compost, I want to say a word about our heap. Some of you may have noticed that it no longer smells. This is thanks to a rota of eight volunteers who turn it twice a week and keep the balance of green and brown just right. It is, I am told, a science. I am also told it is not a topic for dinner parties. I disagree. I think it is the most interesting thing in the garden.

By the end of May, every bed was full. We had planted over three thousand seedlings and sown more than two hundred rows of seed. The garden was a sea of green, with the first small flowers beginning to show.

It was the sort of sight that makes all the cold mornings worth it.

Now, none of this could happen without the people who do the work, and so it is time to turn to them.

## Our volunteers

We have one hundred and sixty-three registered volunteers.

I want you to hold that number in your head for a moment, because it is quite extraordinary. In a town of this size, one person in every hundred and fifty gives some of their time to this garden.

Of those, about forty come regularly, every week or every other week. They are the backbone. They know where the hoses are kept, which tap sticks, and which wheelbarrow has a wobble.

Another hundred or so come when they can. They turn up for a big job, a work day, a festival or a crisis. They are the muscle, and also the laughter.

And the rest are what I call our seasonal friends. They arrive in the spring full of enthusiasm, they disappear in August when it gets hot, and they come back at harvest to ask what happened to their beans. We love them too.

What I notice every year is how different our volunteers are. We have teenagers who need hours for their awards. We have retired teachers, nurses, plumbers and bus drivers. We have people who have never held a trowel, and people who could name every apple tree in the country.

We have a group from the care home down the road, who come on Tuesday afternoons with their support workers and sit in the sunshine pricking out seedlings. I asked one of them last summer what she liked best about it. She said, it is the first time in years that anyone has asked me for my advice.

That is not a small thing. That is what a garden does.

We also have a growing number of people who are looking for work, or for a fresh start. Some come through the job centre, some through the probation service, and some simply because a friend told them about us. They bring skills we did not know we needed. One of them rebuilt our whole irrigation system in a fortnight. Another painted the mural on the side of the tool store. Another just brought good company on bad days.

Now, I would like to thank a few people by name, and I will do it quickly, because I could do this all night.

Thank you to Desmond, who has opened the gates at seven every Saturday morning for six years without missing one. Not a single one. Not even the morning after his daughter's wedding.

Thank you to the three Abubakar sisters, who run our plant sale and have never once let a customer leave without a second pot of something.

Thank you to Tomasz, who keeps the beehives and who has taught half the town not to panic when a bee lands on them.

Thank you to Helen, who writes our newsletter, and who is the only person I know who can make a report on drainage sound like a thriller.

Thank you to the young people from the secondary school, who built the new compost bays as part of a project, and who have asked me to point out that they are still waiting for the certificates. I have not forgotten. They are coming.

And thank you, most of all, to the ones who do not want to be named. You know who you are. You are the ones who stay behind to lock up, who pick up the litter, who fill the water butts, who say nothing and simply do.

We could not run this place without you.

Now, I want to be honest about something. Volunteering is not always easy. People are busy. People get tired. People move away, or change jobs, or have babies, or hurt their backs. Every year we lose some of our best helpers, and every year we have to find new ones.

This year, we tried to make that easier.

We introduced a simple welcome pack for new volunteers, with a map, a list of jobs and a contact name. We started a buddy system, so that nobody has to walk through the gate alone on their first day. And we set up a shared calendar, so that people can see at a glance where help is needed, and sign up for just one hour if that is all they have.

The result was a rise of almost a third in new sign-ups, and a drop in the number of people who drift away after the first month. I think those little things matter. A friendly face, a clear job and a cup of tea will do more than any grand plan.

If you are here tonight and you have been thinking about joining us, this is your sign. Come on Saturday. Bring boots. We will find you something to do, and I promise it will not involve the compost heap unless you ask.

## The money

Now we come to the part of the evening that some of you have been dreading, and some of you have been waiting for. Let us talk about money.

I will keep this simple. Our treasurer, Gloria, will take you through the detail in a moment, and the full accounts are on the table at the back. But I want to give you the shape of it first.

Last year, we took in just over forty-eight thousand pounds. We spent a little under forty-six thousand. So we ended the year with a small surplus of about two thousand three hundred pounds.

That may not sound like much, but I want you to understand how remarkable it is. Three years ago, we were running at a loss, and we were weeks away from having to close the plant sale and cut our opening hours.

So where does the money come from?

About a third of it comes from grants. These are applications we write to charities and the council, and they are a lot of work, and I want to thank the small team who do them. A grant is never a gift. It is something we earn, with forms and reports and photographs of smiling children.

About a quarter comes from sales. That is plants, produce, honey, jam, and the famous chutney. The chutney alone brought in over three thousand pounds. If you have not tried it, you are missing out. If you have tried it, you will know why we cannot make it fast enough.

About a fifth comes from donations and memberships. We have three hundred and ten paying members, and every one of them is a voice that says, this matters.

And the rest comes from events, from workshops, from hiring out our meeting room, and from small gifts left in a box by the gate.

Now, where does it go?

Almost half of it goes on people. That means our two part-time staff, our garden coordinator and our learning officer, along with training and a modest amount for expenses. We never forget that without those two, the volunteer system would fall apart.

A quarter goes on the garden itself. Seeds, compost, tools, water, repairs and insurance. It is surprising how quickly a spade can wear out when forty people use it.

A tenth goes on the building and the greenhouse project, which I will come to. And the remainder goes on admin, on printing, on the website and on the odd emergency.

I want to say plainly: we are not rich. We are careful. And we are fortunate to have a treasurer who sends me a message if I buy a roll of tape without asking.

There is one more number I would like you to remember.

For every pound we spend, we estimate that we give back about four pounds in value to the town. We worked this out with the help of a student from the university, who counted the vegetables given away, the hours of volunteer time, the visits from schools and the savings on health and wellbeing.

That is not a precise figure. But it is a hopeful one.

Before I hand over to Gloria, I want to answer a question that people often ask me in the queue for tea. They say, why do you not just charge for entry?

The answer is simple. This garden belongs to everyone. The moment we put up a barrier, we change what it is. We would rather ask for help and trust people to give it, and so far, the town has never let us down.

Gloria, over to you.

## The new greenhouse

And now, the news that I have been dying to share.

After four years of planning, three failed applications, two changes of design and one very damp meeting, we have our greenhouse.

I say this with a slightly shaky voice, because there were nights when I thought it would never happen.

Let me remind you where we started. Our old greenhouse was a lean-to against the shed, made of old windows and hope. It was draughty. It leaked. It was home to a family of mice who had, I believe, formed a tenants' association.

We could grow about two hundred seedlings in it, in a good year. And those seedlings were all fighting for space with the lawnmower.

So we dreamed bigger.

The new building is nine metres long and four metres wide, with a south-facing roof of twin-wall panels and a low brick wall on the north side to hold the heat. It has a proper floor, with drains. It has benches at three heights, so that children and wheelchair users can reach them. And it has a rainwater system that collects from the whole roof and stores it in two big tanks at the back.

It cost thirty-one thousand pounds. Eighteen thousand came from a lottery grant, six thousand from the council, four thousand from local businesses, and the last three thousand from you.

I want to say that again. Three thousand pounds came from people in this room, in coins and notes and bank transfers, because you believed in it.

I remember the day we opened the collecting tin in the spring. Gloria counted it twice, because she thought she had made a mistake. One of the notes was a fifty. It had a little drawing of a tomato on it in felt pen. I do not know who put it there. Whoever you are, thank you.

The building work was done mostly by volunteers, guided by a local builder who gave us his weekends at cost. We dug the foundations in the rain. We laid the bricks in the sun. We argued about the position of the door for two whole Saturdays, and in the end, we put it in the right place anyway.

It is not quite finished. The heating is still to come, and we are saving up for the automatic vents. But it is up, it is dry and it is full of life.

This spring, we expect to raise over two thousand seedlings in it, ten times more than before. We will sell a good number to the public, and we will give away many more to the school gardens and to families who join our Grow at Home scheme.

We are also planning to use it in the winter, for salad leaves and herbs, and for a small cut-flower business that we hope will bring in a steady income.

And we have a bigger dream. We want the greenhouse to be a place where people can learn. We want to run evening classes there, and weekend workshops, and a monthly club for the over-sixties. We want it to be warm on a cold day and green on a grey one.

I would like to invite you all to see it after the meeting. It is just a short walk across the yard. Bring a torch, and watch your step, because the path is not yet finished.

I promise you this. When you walk in and smell the warm soil and the damp leaves, you will know every hour was worth it.

## Schools and learning

I would like to turn now to the youngest members of our community, who are also, without question, the loudest.

This year, nine hundred and forty children visited the garden on school trips. That is more than double the number from two years ago, and I want to explain how it happened.

It began with a simple idea from our learning officer, Kwame. He said, why do we wait for the schools to call us? Let us go and knock on the door.

So he did. He walked into six primary schools with a bucket of soil and a jar of worms, and he asked the head teachers one question. Would you like your class to grow something?

Every single one said yes.

Since then, we have run what we call the Seed to Supper programme. Each class gets a planter box for the school playground, a packet of seeds and a visit from Kwame once a fortnight. In the spring, the class comes to the garden, plants out their seedlings and meets the people who look after them. In the autumn, they come again to harvest, and we cook what they have grown on a camp stove under the orchard trees.

I have been to several of those meals. I have seen a seven-year-old who claimed to hate vegetables eat three helpings of roasted beetroot, because it was her beetroot. I have seen a boy who rarely speaks stand on a chair and explain to a whole class how a bean plant knows which way is up.

These are the moments that do not show up on a spreadsheet.

But there are numbers, too. Teachers have told us that attendance on garden days is nearly perfect. They tell us that pupils who find the classroom hard are often the ones who shine outdoors. And they tell us that parents are asking for seeds and for recipes, which means the garden is quietly travelling into kitchens all over the town.

We have also begun a partnership with the secondary school, where a small group of older students are earning a qualification in horticulture. They spend one afternoon a week with us, learning to prune, to graft and to read the soil. Two of them have already been offered apprenticeships with the council parks team. I could not be prouder.

And we have not forgotten the grown-ups. Our weekend workshops, on subjects from pickling to pruning, sold out almost every time. Over three hundred adults took part. The most popular session, I am told, was the one on making your own bird feeders. The least popular was the one on drainage. I am sorry, Helen.

Learning in a garden is different from learning in a classroom. Nobody sits still, nobody is graded and nobody is wrong for long, because the plants will tell you. You plant too deep, and they do not come up. You water too much, and they sulk. You get it right, and they simply grow, and no one has to say well done.

There is a kind of quiet confidence in that, and I think we need more of it.

Next year, we hope to reach every primary school in the town, and to begin a toddler session on Thursday mornings for parents and carers. If you know a family with small children, please tell them. Wellies are provided.

## Challenges and lessons

I promised you honesty tonight, so I want to say a few words about what went wrong.

The first thing is water.

You will remember that August was the hottest month on record in this part of the country. For twenty-three days in a row, there was no rain at all. The mains supply was restricted, and for nine of those days we were told we could not use a hose.

Our water butts held enough for about four days. After that, we were carrying watering cans from the pond.

I will never forget the sight of forty volunteers forming a human chain from the pond to the vegetable beds, passing buckets hand to hand in the evening light. It was a bit ridiculous, and a bit beautiful.

We saved most of the crops. But we lost the entire bed of lettuce, half of the courgettes and a lot of sleep.

The lesson is clear. We cannot rely on the tap. The new greenhouse tanks will help, and we have already ordered four more large butts for the main garden. We are also experimenting with a thick mulch of straw on every bed, which holds the water in the ground and keeps the roots cool. Early results are very promising.

The second challenge was pests. Slugs, as I promised, deserve a chapter of their own. This was the worst year for them since we started. They ate seedlings, they ate lettuce, and on one memorable night they ate an entire row of sunflowers down to the stalks.

We tried beer traps, which work, but smell. We tried eggshells, which do not work, but look pretty. We tried a patrol of volunteers with torches on damp evenings, which worked very well, but was not popular.

In the end, the best defence was to invite more wildlife. The new pond brought in frogs and toads, and the hedgehog houses that Tomasz built are now almost full. I cannot prove that this made a difference. But the slug damage in the autumn was far less than in the spring, and I like to think the hedgehogs were doing their bit.

The third challenge was people.

Running a garden with so many volunteers is joyful, and it is also hard. People have different ideas about what should be planted, where the paths should go and whether the compost heap is a feature or an eyesore. There were a few difficult meetings this year, and there was one week in July when I seriously considered moving to a flat with no garden at all.

What helped was agreeing a few simple rules. We decided that every big decision would be written down. We decided that anyone could raise a concern, and that it would be heard within two weeks. And we decided that when we disagree, we will say so kindly, in daylight, and over tea.

It sounds small. It has made an enormous difference.

If there is a single lesson I take from this year, it is that a garden is not a project to be finished. It is a relationship to be kept. It will surprise you, it will disappoint you, and if you keep showing up, it will repay you in ways you cannot predict.

## Plans for next year

So, where do we go from here?

I have a short list, and I would like your help with every item on it.

The first priority is to finish the greenhouse. We need about five thousand pounds for the heating and the automatic vents. We have already raised one thousand two hundred, so we are a quarter of the way there. A few of you have already offered to run a sponsored bake, and a local brewery has promised to donate the proceeds of a tasting evening in March. If you would like to help, please see Gloria after the meeting.

The second priority is to become more resilient. That means better water storage, more shade and more varieties that cope with a changing climate. We will plant a drought-tolerant border along the south wall, and we will trial a few unusual crops, including chickpeas, okra and a hardy kind of fig. If they work, you will taste them in the autumn. If they do not, we will blame the weather.

Third, we want to open the garden more often. At the moment, we are open on Saturdays and on Wednesday afternoons. From April, we hope to add Sunday mornings, and a quiet hour on Friday evenings for people who find crowds difficult. To do this, we need about twelve more volunteers who are happy to act as hosts. The work is simple. You say hello, you point people to the tea, and you tell them about the frogs.

Fourth, we want to share what we have learned. We have been asked by two neighbouring towns how we did it, and I would love to run a day for other community gardens, with talks, tours and a big lunch. We could call it Grow Together. If you have a better name, I am open to offers.

Fifth, we want to look after one another. We will begin a small hardship fund, so that any volunteer who is struggling can ask for help with travel, with food or with a bit of breathing space. It is a small thing, but it feels right.

And finally, we want to keep a bit of wildness. I know that sounds odd for a garden, but I believe a place that is too tidy is a place that has lost something. So we will leave a corner of the orchard unmown, we will let the nettles have a patch by the fence, and we will keep the old log pile for the beetles. If anyone complains, I will send them to speak to the beetles.

None of these plans is certain. All of them need money, time and patience. But I have learned over nine years that this community has plenty of all three.

So I will ask you now for one small promise. Before you leave tonight, find one thing on this list, and decide how you can help with it. It might be an hour, a donation, a skill, or just a word to a neighbour. Write it on one of the cards at the door and put it in the box.

Gardens do not grow by themselves. They grow because we choose, over and over again, to look after them.

## Thank you

I am nearly at the end, and I know you are ready for your tea.

Let me close with a short story.

Last October, on a grey and drizzly afternoon, I was alone in the garden closing up. I was tired. I had a cold. I was thinking about the long list of jobs still undone, and wondering, as I sometimes do, whether any of it was worth the trouble.

I was sweeping the path by the pond when a small boy came through the gate. He was perhaps five. He was holding his grandfather's hand and carrying a paper bag.

He walked straight up to me and said, are you the garden lady?

I said, I suppose I am.

He opened the bag, and inside was a tiny pumpkin, no bigger than an orange. He said, I grew this in a pot at home. I took your seeds from the shed. I want to give it to the garden, so it can have babies.

I did not know what to say. I think I said thank you three times. His grandfather smiled, and the two of them went home, and I stood there in the rain holding a very small pumpkin.

That pumpkin is on my kitchen windowsill. I am saving the seeds, and next spring I am going to plant them in the very first bed by the gate.

I tell you this because it explains better than any chart why we do what we do. A garden is a gift that goes round and round. Someone plants, someone tends, someone eats, someone saves a seed, and someone plants again. Nobody owns it, and everybody can join.

So tonight, I want to say thank you.

Thank you to our staff, who work harder than their hours allow.

Thank you to our trustees, who give their evenings and their good sense.

Thank you to our funders and our neighbours, who believed in a patch of broken concrete.

Thank you to our volunteers, every single one, from the Saturday regulars to the one-time visitors who pulled a few weeds and went home happy.

Thank you to the children, who remind us what it is all for.

And thank you to you, who came on a wet night, and who will be here again in the spring with a spade.

Please enjoy the tea, the biscuits and the company. Come and see the greenhouse. Ask your questions, even the ones about slugs. And if you are not yet a member, there are forms on the table by the door.

Goodnight, and thank you.

Now, let us go and look at the greenhouse.
